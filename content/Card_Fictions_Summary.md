---
title: Finger Flicker
difficulty: advanced
props: [cards]
tags: [cards, crimp, jog-shuffle, precision, blindfold]
source: "Card Fictions, Pit Hartling"
---
Effect: A shuffled deck is placed on the table. A spectator names a number between ten and twenty. With a flick of just one finger, the performer kicks exactly that many cards off the tabled deck. For a repeat, a higher number is named, a spectator covers the performer's eyes, and the performer repeats the feat left-handed and blindfolded — once again with absolute precision. For the finale, a card is selected and shuffled back into the deck. After briefly riffling through the cards, the performer places the deck squarely onto the table, remembers the selection's position, and flicks directly to the named card.

Method summary:

Set-up:
- Put a breather crimp into the King of Hearts. Hold the card face-up, pinch it between the thumb (above) and second and third fingers (below) at one corner, squeeze tightly, and pull the fingers across to the diagonally opposite corner. Repeat along the other diagonal, resulting in a crosswise crimp. The slight bump faces the back of the card; when the deck is cut face-down, the crimped King will be the top card of the lower half.
- Edge-mark the crimped card (a nail nick suffices for impromptu use) to facilitate locating it in the squared deck.
- Position the crimped card eighteenth from the top of the deck.

Method and Presentation:
Hold the deck in the right hand in position for an overhand shuffle. *(Ask a spectator to name a number between ten and twenty. Time this request so she answers before your shuffle reaches the break — you can imperceptibly slow down if needed.)* Take a block of at least eighteen cards with the left thumb. **Run the next card singly, injogging it**, and shuffle off the rest. Take a break under the injog and start shuffling to the break. If she names seventeen (the original number above the crimp), simply **throw to the break and throw the remaining packet on top**. If a lower number is named, **subtract it from seventeen, throw to the break, run the necessary small number of cards, then throw the rest on top**. If eighteen or nineteen is named, **do not throw to the break; stop the shuffle with one or two cards still on top before throwing**.

Place the deck on the table with a long side parallel to the table edge. Position your right hand so the first finger almost touches the deck's inner long side. **Kick the first finger against the edge of the cards and upwards** — all cards above the crimp turn face-up and land in front of the bottom half. Count the cards slowly and clearly to verify.

*(For the repeat: position the crimp twenty-eighth from the top, have a number between twenty and thirty named, and repeat the same jog-shuffle handling. Announce you will do it left-handed, have a spectator cover your eyes, and let the spectator count the cards.)*

*(For the finale: force the crimped King of Hearts via a dribble force.)* After the spectator shuffles the selection back in, retrieve the cards and cut to leave the breather near the centre. **Pause, and without comment riffle up the inner end of the deck**, pretending to remember the order as the cards fly by. In fact, simply remember the card above the selection. When the spectator names her card (King of Hearts), say, almost to yourself, the name of the card you just remembered. After a dramatic pause, **do the Flick** — the card that shows on the face of the cut-off packet is the card you mentioned. Dramatically turn over the next card: the King of Hearts.

Key Notes:
- The breather crimp allows the deck to be flicked cleanly, cutting off exactly all cards above it.
- For the Ambidextrous Flick variation, use an additional deck in memorized order (also containing a breather). Have two spectators each name a number. Cross your arms and flick both decks simultaneously.
- The overhand shuffle method for positioning the crimp is also used in Triple Countdown (page 68).

Credits: Bears resemblance to the classic salt-location. Versions by John Scarne: "Hands-Off Miracle" (p.61) and "The Spirit Card Trick" (p.237) in Scarne on Card Tricks (1950). Harry Lorayne's "Salt-Less" (Close-Up Card Magic, 1976) uses a crimp instead of salt. The overhand shuffle positioning was inspired by a force in Annemann's 202 Methods Of Forcing (1933); Pit Hartling added the Jog Shuffle.

---
title: Master of the Mess
difficulty: advanced
props: [cards]
tags: [cards, faro, triumph-plot, chaos-shuffle]
source: "Card Fictions, Pit Hartling"
---
Effect: The performer challenges himself to perform with the deck in a complete mess: all fifty-two cards are splashed onto the table, forming a haphazard mix of face-up and face-down cards. The audience concentrates on one card. Without asking a single question, the performer removes cards one by one from the heap, gradually eliminating all but a single card — incredibly, the selection. A new card is then selected and lost in the mess. The performer carefully squeezes the deck at the fingertips — chaos turns into order: every card lies face-down again, except the selection.

Method summary:

Set-up:
- A complete deck of fifty-two cards in good condition.

Method and Presentation:
*("Sometimes, to surprise myself, I do something in a very different way than usual. Recently, I wondered whether I could still perform magic with the deck in a complete mess, like this...")* Turn about half the deck face-up and **faro-shuffle this packet into the face-down rest without pushing the halves together**. Place the deck onto the table in its incomplete-faro condition and give it a twist, letting the cards spin on each other (the "Chaos Shuffle"). Keep turning and spreading until the cards are distributed haphazardly across the table. Invite two or three spectators to join in, mixing everything with both hands.

Effect One — Divination:
Push the cards together and square the deck. Note and remember the bottom face-down card (e.g., Eight of Hearts). **Force this card with a peek force: swing cut about half the deck into the left hand, pull the top card of the left-hand packet slightly towards yourself with the left thumb, drop the right-hand half on top, and push the protruding card in until it remains injogged only a fraction. The Eight of Hearts is directly above this injog. Hold the deck at the lower left corner and run your right first finger across the upper right corner, asking a spectator to say stop — stop at the injog (you will feel a slight click).** Show the card to the spectator.

Briefly repeat the "Chaos Shuffle" — faro-style push and twist — spreading the cards out again, with the selection face-up and clearly exposed. *(Turn your back: "If you can see your card in the mess, point to it silently to inform the others. If not, point to any other card.")* This is a bluff — you know she sees her selection and will point to it.

Begin **"intuitive elimination"**: remove cards from the centre, singly and in small groups, dropping them onto a discard pile. Avoid the Eight of Hearts throughout. Meanwhile, **secretly arrange the discard pile into a 2-2-1-2-1 face-up/face-down sequence** (think in groups of four, alternating between "2-2" and "1-2-1"). After eliminating about half the deck, remix the remaining cards, turn the selection face-down, and track its position. Continue eliminating until five cards remain. Turn all five face-up in a line with the selection second from the right. **Take the card from the right end and use it to scoop up the selection. Turning your wrist, turn both cards face-down — push the indifferent card from the back onto the discards and immediately place the selection back face-down at the right end.** Without pausing, continue eliminating the remaining cards in a flowing rhythm, saying: *"It's not the Eight, not the Ten, and none of these two either!"* One card remains on the table. When the spectator names the thought-of card, appear irritated, then reveal the last card — the selection.

During the spectators' reaction, **calmly give the deck one straddle faro** (cut off 25 cards and shuffle them into the remaining 26). Place the selection on top of the deck face-down, **execute a second faro without pushing the halves together**, and repeat the twist-flourish (the "Chaos Shuffle") — the deck appears to still be a complete mess.

Effect Two — Order:
Square the deck. **Dribble the cards and have a spectator call out stop — stop in the small face-down block just above centre.** Hold a break above the selection. **Exchange the selection with the top card via an overhand shuffle: peel off only the top card, throw all cards above the break on top, run the selection singly, run a few more single cards, then throw the rest on top slightly outjogged. Start a second shuffle by taking the top half, run the same number of single cards, and throw the rest on top.** Turn the whole deck over (the selection ends face-up on the bottom). **Openly give the deck one last out-faro**, letting the cards "sink" together. **Aided by natural breaks, take out the centre block of twenty-six cards and place it on top, keeping a break between the other two quarters. Cut at that break** — the face-up and face-down cards are now separated. **Secretly reverse the bottom half** of the deck.

*("After all that crazy shuffling and mixing...")* **Hold the deck at the fingertips and very delicately, ever so slowly, square the edges. Dramatically crack the deck between your fingers.** Very slowly spread the deck on the table — card after card comes into sight face-down, until one single face-up card appears: the selection.

Key Notes:
- The two effects are methodologically linked; the first effect contains a large part of the second effect's method. Done correctly, spectators perceive them as separate and independent.
- Keep the discard pile in the "shadow zone" — lean slightly to one side, establishing the centre of the table as centre stage.
- The twist-flourish is a memorable "anchor" — the same handling used in very different situations brings back the image of chaos.
- When you cannot do a perfect faro, the shortcut of cutting out the centre twenty-six cards achieves the same result.
- Timing of the climax is crucial: treat the revelation of the palmed/missing card and the Royal-Flush-like straightening as one extended climax, not as an initial effect plus a kicker.

Credits: The basic idea was independently conceived by others. Ed Marlo's "76-76-67-67" (Faro Notes, 1958, p.29). Camilo Vázquez's "Un Gran Triunfo" uses the same faro-based grouping of reversed cards (in Tamariz, Sinfonia En Mnemonica Mayor Vol 1, 2000, p.176). Inspired by Dai Vernon's "Triumph" (Stars of Magic). Special thanks to Denis Behr for his invaluable input during the routine's evolution.

---
title: Colour Sense
difficulty: advanced
props: [cards]
tags: [cards, memorization, mentalism, pattern-principle]
source: "Card Fictions, Pit Hartling"
---
Effect: A deck of cards is thoroughly shuffled by a spectator, who then takes a packet and holds it under the table face-up. The performer touches the table surface with his palm directly above the hidden packet and feels for the colours. One by one, he correctly names the colour of each card before the spectator places it onto the table. He can also identify picture cards, tell the number of cards remaining, and finally even sense the exact suit and value of the last card.

Method summary:

Method Overview: When the shuffled deck is retrieved, the performer briefly spreads the cards from hand to hand and memorizes the colour-sequence of the first thirteen cards, using the cover of "Performing Mode" — apparently setting the stage while absent-mindedly looking at the cards. The deck is roughly divided into quarters and the memorized packet psychologically forced on a spectator to be taken under the table.

Set-up:
- No special preparation. A shuffled deck is used.
- Optional: when retrieving the shuffled deck, cut so that the sixth card from the face is the first picture card (for an additional presentational touch).

Method and Presentation:
*("At the moment I practice seeing through walls. I have only just begun and it's more feeling than actually seeing, but I can already make out shadowy outlines and even slight hints of colour...")* Retrieve the shuffled deck and hold it face-up in left-hand dealing position. *("Ok, let's see, we need some space here...")* As you gesture with the right hand, **the left thumb pushes over the top two cards**, allowing you to see and remember the first triplet. Using **Lewis Jones' Pattern Principle**: mentally divide the colour sequence into triplets. There are eight possible red/black combinations; each is given a unique, easily remembered label (e.g., RBB = Bottom, BRR = Upper, BRB = Middle, RRR = All). Remember only the first letter of each triplet, forming a short word (e.g., BUMA). Two such words securely store the colour sequence of twenty-four cards — about twice what is needed. As spectators clear space, continue "absent-mindedly" spreading the deck face-up — *("Hm, well... I don't know... how shall we do it...")* — memorizing the colours and also the suit and value of the thirteenth card.

*("Ah, I know!")* Suddenly sit up, square the deck taking a break under the thirteenth card from the face. Take the memorized packet and place it on the table face-down. **Divide the remaining cards into three more rough quarters**, placing them to the left and right so the memorized packet is second from the right. Rest your left hand behind the second packet from the left and say: *"Take one packet and hold it under the table"* — the left hand blocks the far-left packet and visually emphasises the force packet as the centre of the first three. If the spectator takes any other packet, simply have more spectators take packets until the memorized packet is taken (the others serve as demo-piles).

**Switch back to Performing Mode.** *("Ok, you shuffled the deck and took some cards under the table. Very good.")* Place your right hand on the table above the spectator's packet, palm touching the tabletop. Move your hand around faith-healer-style. After a pause: *"Oh — can you turn the packet face-up, please?"* Name each colour one by one, having the spectator place cards face-up onto the table. Vary the rhythm — difficulty with one card, immediate naming of the next. If the optional picture-card setup is used: at the sixth card, name the wrong colour and immediately correct yourself: *"The next one is... black — ah no, red!"* — the card appears and is the first picture card, making a very memorable moment.

After nine cards are correctly named (three triplets), four remain. *("I can see everything! All the cards under the table. Very easy! Do you still have some? Yes? How many?")* — this almost always induces someone to challenge you to feel for the number. Pretend to count through the table: *"Ah yes, that's one, two, three... four... four more cards!"* Name the colours of the next three. For the last card, go "all the way": have the spectator press it against the table from below, feel through the table, and hesitantly name first the colour, then suit and value.

Key Notes:
- Lewis Jones' Pattern Principle: eight possible triplets each receive a unique label. The first letters of four triplets form a short word. Two such words store the colour sequence of twenty-four cards.
- Karl Fulves' "Combo" (1998) is another practical method for memorizing colour sequences.
- The "Performing Mode" concept is essential here: while memorizing, you appear to be setting the stage and thinking about how to proceed, not performing. Once you switch back, spectators have already dismissed those moments as unimportant.
- The psychological force of one packet from four: place the left hand behind the second-from-left packet (blocking it), say "take one packet" rather than "choose any packet", and immediately continue with instructions, leaving little time for conscious decision.
- The "picture card" touch: deliberately making a mistake and correcting yourself when the first picture card appears is a powerful convincer.

Credits: Thanks to Michael Weber, who showed the author a similar routine during a late-night session in Japan. Pit Hartling's changes: the exact handling of memorizing (using Performing Mode as cover), the induced challenge near the end, the picture-card touch, and the notion of feeling the cards through a table. Lewis Jones generously allowed inclusion of his "Pattern Principle". The psychological force detail was first pointed out by Helge Thun. Linguistic strategies can be found in Kenton Knepper's Wonder Words and the work of Juan Tamariz.

---
title: High Noon
difficulty: advanced
props: [cards]
tags: [cards, card-under-watch, dribble-force, top-change]
source: "Card Fictions, Pit Hartling"
---
Effect: In a dramatic duel, the performer manages to snatch a selected card from a spectator's hand. Not only does he achieve this in lightning speed, he also finds time to fold the card neatly in quarters and sneak it under the spectator's watch.

Method summary:

Set-up:
- Neatly fold one duplicate card in quarters, face outwards. Keep it in a pocket.
- Cut the corresponding card to the top of the deck.

Method and Presentation:
**Secretly obtain the folded duplicate in your right hand, clipped between the first and second fingers.** Announce that you will stage a "duel." Ask one spectator to hold out his hand and the other spectators to stand in a circle, forming an impromptu arena. As you pull your opponent closer towards yourself, **secretly load the folded card under his watch by sliding the clipped card under the watch as you pull him towards you.** The main cover is that you have not yet switched to Performing Mode — you are just organizing the contest and setting the stage.

With the audience surrounding you, cut the deck and establish an angle jog (or break) above the former top card. **Execute a dribble force**, dribbling the cards onto the spectator's hand and asking him to stop you whenever he likes. Let all cards below the jog drop onto his hand. Hand the rest to another spectator. Show the top card, emphasising that you will not peek, then put it back.

**Briefly pick up all the cards from his hand.** Ask him to keep his hand there as a table — this prevents him from dropping his hand and prematurely revealing the folded card. **Apparently lose the selection anywhere in the packet by adding five cards on top using a jog shuffle: undercut half, run five cards on top of the selection, injog the next and shuffle off. Establish a break under the injog, shuffle off to this break, and throw the rest on top.** The selection is now sixth from the top.

*("You are going to see one card after the other placed onto your hand. As soon as your selection is on your hand, slap your other hand on top as quickly as you can. I try to sneak the card away. You know the card — that's your advantage — but I have years of training.")* First, a **rehearsal**: place the first card delicately onto his hand and ask him to slam his other hand on top. Triple purpose: he overcomes inhibitions about really hitting, the other spectators see what this is about, and it builds the conflict. *("Oh, you're fast! Let me try this with someone else!")*

The game starts for real. Clearly show card after card and place them onto his hand. **For the fourth card, do a double turnover**, mentioning the name: *"The Two of Diamonds, maybe?"* Turn the double face-down and place the top card onto his hand. **The next card is a double again and the selection will show.** Place the top card onto his hand and — WHAM! — his hand crashes down.

*("Ah, that was close... I am glad I got it at all!")* The first part sounds like a weak excuse; the second causes irritation. Point to his hands: *"Ten of Spades, right?"* — the sentence and gesture are purposely ambiguous. He looks: instead of the Ten of Spades, he finds the Two of Diamonds.

Give spectators ample time to appreciate this first climax. *("I sneaked your card out just before you could get it, and then I folded it exactly in half. Then, I folded it in half again. It took me about twenty milliseconds. Did you look at your watch?")* After a final pause: *"Look at your watch."*

Key Notes:
- The rehearsal phase is crucial: ensure the spectator genuinely commits to hitting fast and hard.
- Placing cards on the spectator's hand rather than the table: avoids spilling drinks and silverware, produces a better WHACK sound, and enables performing without a table (allowing a group to gather around for an "arena" feel).
- The conflict is about the situation, not the spectator: emphasise "years of practice" and that nobody has ever beaten you — this takes the sting out of the personal aspect.
- The watch-load solves the classic "Reflex" reproduction problem: the strong reaction to the vanish does not weaken the final revelation, because the card was hidden before the duel even began.
- The ambiguous statement "Ten of Spades, right?" while pointing to his hands effectively says two things: "Your card was the Ten" and "The Ten is between your hands."

Credits: Core effect by Paul Harris: "Reflex" (first described by Michael Ammar, Magical Arts Journal; full description in Paul Harris, The Art of Astonishment Vol.3, "Whack Your Pack", 1996). The combination with "Card Under the Spectator's Watch" was developed together with Andreas Buchty; Marc Kanert also experimented along similar lines. Similar control handling by Christian Scherer in "Reflexartig" (Karten à la carte, 1997). Card-under-watch effect previously by Norman Beck (Genii Magazine, Vol. 58 No. 7, May 1995).

---
title: Cincinnati Pit
difficulty: advanced
props: [cards]
tags: [cards, gambling-demonstration, stacking, faro, false-shuffle]
source: "Card Fictions, Pit Hartling"
---
Effect: In answer to the question "Could you cheat in an actual game of poker?", the performer gives a demonstration of card control. A freely named four-of-a-kind is openly removed from a shuffled deck. In less than ten seconds, these four cards are stacked to fall to the performer's imaginary partner in a four-handed game. The true dimensions of the feat are only fully realized when the performer reveals he also managed to get two more players interested in the pot by dealing them the four Queens and the four Kings. The performer rakes in all chips with his winning Poker of Aces.

Method summary:

Set-up:
- The Queens and Kings are at the back of the deck in any order. This can easily be reached by culling the eight cards between tricks.

Method and Presentation:
Hand out packets of cards to several spectators for shuffling. **Give the first nine cards to a first spectator.** Let the others shuffle their packets. *("You can also arrange the cards any way you like. Hey, you can try and build your phone number if you want to!")* As you say this, take back the nine-card packet and fan it with faces towards yourself. **Quickly arrange them in Q, K, Q, K, Q, K, Q, K, X order** (X being an indifferent card on the face; "ladies first" as a mnemonic) from back to face. *("Or just shuffle, it's all up to you!")* Immediately **false-shuffle the packet and throw it face-up onto the table** — the indifferent card shows. Have the other spectators throw their packets on top, completing the deck.

Ask for a number between one and ten (say, seven). Spread the deck face-up and **place the four Sevens out onto the table as you come to them, secretly culling the Aces to the back** (Hofzinser-style cull). Intentionally pass one Seven early to avoid removing all four before culling all Aces. At the end, with only three Sevens on the table, spread through and clearly push out the "overlooked" last Seven — this leaves a very clean image of the open removal of the four-of-a-kind.

*("During a short shuffle, I will control the four-of-a-kind to fall to my confederate's hand — in less than ten seconds.")* Pick up the deck and nonchalantly **shuffle the top four cards (the Aces) to the bottom**. **Introduce the Sevens into the deck as a block somewhat below centre, angling them towards the left to obtain a break. Step all cards above the break towards the left and execute a pressure fan**, apparently showing the four-of-a-kind lost somewhere in the middle. **When closing the fan, the step is still intact.** Place the deck on the table with long sides towards you, ready for a riffle shuffle.

**Locate the step and cut all cards above it towards the left.** Situation check: the right half has four Aces on the bottom and the named four-of-a-kind on top. On top of the left half are the Queens and Kings in alternating order.

Have someone time you. *"Go!"* **Start a regular riffle shuffle. Hold back the last four cards on the left. Continue riffling on the right and hold back the top three cards from that side. Let two cards fall from the left thumb, one from the right, the remaining two from the left, and finally the last two from the right.** Do NOT yet square. **Execute a Zarrow-type action below the top stacked cards**: with your thumbs, push the top cards slightly towards the spectators, execute the actions of the Zarrow Shuffle, secretly separating the two halves except the stacked cards. Push the halves together, holding a break.

**For a second shuffle, cut the half above the break towards the right. Leave at least four cards from the right half (these are the Aces) and start a regular shuffle.** Again, riffle the left half faster. Hold back the last four cards from the left, hold back only the last card from the right. Let two cards fall from the left, the card from the right, and finally the remaining two from the left. Immediately call out *"Stop!"*

Ask the time-keeper how long you needed. Calmly push the halves together.

**Deal to four players.** First card to an imaginary player on your left, next to someone opposite. **Take the third card, look at it yourself, smile, and triumphantly place it face-up in front of your "confederate" — a Seven!** In the short off-beat, **deal yourself an Ace from the bottom** and continue. Repeat — his second Seven, off-beat bottom-deal a second Ace. Repeat once more. For his fourth card: look at it — the last Seven — but do a silent miscall, stopping short and placing it face-down with the other three. *("Everybody gets five cards.")* Deal another round (bottom-dealing Aces for yourself). His fifth card is wrong. Suddenly smile and turn over the face-down card — the last Seven.

*("I have actually cheated in more ways than suspected...")* Reveal the four Queens in the first player's hand. Before applause, turn to the second player: *("This fellow also cheated — four Kings!")* Finally reveal your own four Aces.

Key Notes:
- The open arrangement of Q/K in the initial packet is covered by the indifferent card on the face and the false shuffle. Adjusting quickly, without looking at your hands for the last few cards, and the casual attitude make it highly deceptive.
- The pressure fan convincingly shows the four-of-a-kind lost, while the step is preserved for the upcoming riffle stack.
- The race-against-time is a dramatic tool — adjust the time limit to what you can comfortably handle.
- Overhand-shuffle handling (suggested by Stefan Sprenger): use "milk/single" actions (take top and bottom cards together, then run a single — repeat four times) instead of riffle stacking. For this version, start the Q/K sequence with a King instead of a Queen.
- Treat the miscall lightly and playfully — let the spectators feel the demonstration is over, so the subsequent Q/K/A revelations pack maximum punch.

Credits: Direct inspiration from Jörg Alexander's "Poker Finale" (Magie, Vol. 2, 1997, p.70), which is in turn based on Juan Tamariz's "Las 3 jugadas de poker" (Magia Potagia, Vol. III, 1984, p.23). Thematically related to Darwin Ortiz's "The Sting" (Cardshark, 1995, p.184). Overhand-shuffle version by Stefan Sprenger.

---
title: Triple Countdown
difficulty: advanced
props: [cards]
tags: [cards, multiple-selection, jog-shuffle, ghost-switch]
source: "Card Fictions, Pit Hartling"
---
Effect: Three cards are selected and lost in the deck. Three spectators each name a number. After a magic gesture, and without the performer touching the deck, the cards are slowly counted face-up onto the table. The three selections are found at exactly the named positions.

Method summary:

Method Overview: All three spectators secretly remember the same card. The shared selection is controlled to one of the three named positions. The three cards shown at the end are the selection and two indifferent cards. As every spectator sees "his" or "her" card among the three, they all confirm the trick's success.

Set-up:
- A shuffled deck is used. Before you start, secretly take a break under the sixteenth card from the top.

Method and Presentation:
**Execute a standard dribble force** to a spectator sitting opposite you: dribble the cards into your waiting left hand, and time your actions so all cards below the break fall as she calls stop. Place the right-hand packet of sixteen cards to the table and simultaneously **establish a break under the top two cards of the left-hand packet. Execute a double-lift**, displaying the top two cards as one, and ask the spectator to remember her selection.

*("Remember your card.")* As the hands briefly come together, **execute Martin Nash's Ghost Switch**: the double card is held at the right long side between the right fingers below and thumb above. As the right hand approaches the deck, the right fingers push the card from the face of the double to the left while the right thumb pulls the top card to the right. The bottom card is clipped to the top of the deck by the heel of the left thumb as the top card snaps off — the card never seems to touch the deck. Leave the apparent selection face-down.

Turn to a spectator on your right: *"Another one for you..."* **Take the new top card off the deck** with the right hand — unknowingly, he sees the same card. Finally, address a third spectator to your left. As you turn towards him, **execute a Top Change** — the right hand holds an indifferent card while the selection has once more returned to the top. Push the apparent next card somewhat to the right and display it.

*("Now let's lose all three cards back in the deck.")* **Replace the sixteen-card packet onto the deck and establish a heel-break by catching some flesh at the base of your left thumb. Relax the left thumb pressure** — the deck opens along its left side. With the left thumb, slightly pull down the bottom part and **insert the selection (the actual one) into that opening from the front** (Rafael Benatar's handling). Riffle down further and insert the second and third cards. Leave all three outjogged briefly, then very slowly square them — the selection is now seventeenth from the top.

**Position the selection at the named number during a short overhand jog shuffle**: take a block of at least seventeen cards, run one singly injogging it, shuffle off. Take a break under the injog and start shuffling to the break. Ask a spectator to name a number between ten and twenty. If she names seventeen, simply throw to the break and throw the rest on top. If a lower number, subtract it from seventeen and run the necessary small number of cards before throwing. If eighteen or nineteen, leave one or two cards above the original top packet before throwing. The selection is now at the named position from the top.

**Hand the deck to another spectator**, instructing him to hold it tightly with both hands like a vice. *("Here's what is going to happen: I will ask you to name three numbers. I will try to put your three cards at exactly these numbers from the top. Not necessarily your card at your number and yours at your number — that depends on which card is closer to what number. But at the end, your three cards must lie exactly at your chosen positions!")* — this wording implies the cards are different, greatly reducing the risk of exposure.

Ask the spectator on your left to name a number. Make her repeat it. **Once she has repeated her number, you are in an extremely powerful situation: the other two spectators can name any numbers from one to fifty-two, and their "selections" will be found at those positions.**

**Magic gesture**: hold your right hand flat next to the deck and slowly move it upwards, making Geiger counter-like clicks with your tongue. Repeat for the second card. For the third, appear dissatisfied and ask the spectator holding the deck to briefly shake it up and down — the shake does the trick. Triumphantly proclaim it's done.

The spectator holding the cards hands the deck to the first spectator. **She counts cards face-up one by one onto the table.** When she reaches one before her named number, stop her and have her place the next card face-down to the side. *(Has anyone seen their card?)* Nobody has. The deck is handed to the second spectator, who continues counting from where the first left off, same procedure — place the card at his number face-down. Third spectator, same procedure. Take the remaining cards and clearly show them all — no selections anywhere.

*("And, of course...")* Show your hands empty and clearly reveal the three face-down cards — each spectator confirms their selection. *("And it only works with fifteen, twenty-seven and thirty-four!" — naming the actual numbers chosen.)*

Key Notes:
- The principle of having multiple spectators select the same card is classic and powerful. Address three spectators sitting somewhat apart, and show each card so only that spectator and perhaps close neighbours can see it. The attitude of "let's first get the preliminaries out of the way" makes the cards seem insignificant.
- The ambiguous wording "not necessarily your card at your number..." is truthful in a double sense — it genuinely "depends on which card is closer to which number" because there is only one card.
- The face-up counting procedure itself reinforces the belief that three different selections exist in the deck.
- Martin Nash's Ghost Switch: the card never appears to contact the deck, making it exceptionally clean.
- Rafael Benatar's break-insertion technique makes inserting cards into a break look completely uncontrolled.
- The magic gesture defines what the effect actually is — in the artistic reality of performance, it is the gesture that moves the cards.

---
title: Unforgettable
difficulty: advanced
props: [cards]
tags: [cards, memorized-deck, mnemonics, faro, bridge-deal]
source: "Card Fictions, Pit Hartling"
---
Effect: Orange juice seems to have an amazing effect on the performer's mnemonic abilities. After a short sip, he can easily remember the sequence of fifty-two thoroughly shuffled playing cards. After more shuffling and more of his personal magic potion, a one-second scan through the deck is enough for him to recall the exact numerical position of every card. Finally, after emptying the glass, the performer offers a practical application: the whole deck is dealt to four players in a game of Bridge and the performer successfully calls out every single card of any chosen hand in correct order.

Method summary:

Set-up:
- Familiarity with a memorized deck is required, but only the first thirteen cards need to be in actual memorized order.
- The deck is set up as follows (from the top): the first thirteen cards of your memorized deck (stack numbers 1–13) in memorized order; the next thirteen (stack numbers 14–26) in any order; the bottom half (stack numbers 27–52) in any order.
- If you do not use a memorized deck: divide the deck into odd and even values (red Kings counting as odd, black Kings as even), memorize the top thirteen cards and their numerical positions.

Method and Presentation:

Phase I — The Sequence:
Take the deck face-up and hand out packets: about ten cards to a spectator on your right, the remaining cards above a centre break to the next (skipping one person), continuing until you keep the last thirteen — your memorized packet. **Give your thirteen a false mix.** *("Also cut the cards.")* Turn to a spectator on your left, demonstrate cutting a few times, and hand him the packet — he will restrict himself to complete cuts. All cards are now in spectators' hands, and everyone appears to be shuffling and cutting freely.

Retrieve the memorized packet and **cut the first card of your stack back to the top**. Retrieve the next packet (stack numbers 14–26) and **faro shuffle the two packets together**, then place this half face-up on the table. Have the remaining spectators throw their shuffled packets on top in any order.

Situation check: the thirteen memorized cards are now every other card from the top, alternating with thirteen shuffled cards. You have a divided deck — the top half contains stack numbers 1–26; the bottom half contains 27–52.

*Dramatically take a sip of orange juice. Pause a few seconds for effect.* **Spread the deck in a very wide ribbon spread** — but as the right hand still spreads, **the left hand is already sweeping cards up again**, so only about ten cards are actually spread at a time, this group moving across the table from left to right. Done swiftly, it seems impossible to recognize any card, let alone remember the order.

**Spread face-up in another wide ribbon from left to right.** Ask a spectator on your left to take out any card, remember it and its exact position, then replace it at any other position. Turn your back as he does this. *(It is preferable that the card is taken from the left, arranged half — a biased spread with the left half wider helps ensure this.)*

Re-spread the deck. There are three possible situations:
1. The moved card is one of the thirteen memorized cards — immediately identifiable, along with its original position.
2. The moved card was between two memorized cards, replaced in the other half — two memorized cards now lie together (a gap where the card was removed), and one "wrong" high-stack-number card appears among low numbers — that's the selection.
3. The moved card was between memorized cards, replaced in the same half — two memorized cards lie together with two other cards between them; a single distinguishing fishing statement reveals all.

After successfully identifying the card and its original position, replace it. The memorized cards are still every second from the top.

Phase II — The Positions:
*("Remembering an order is one thing, remembering exact positions is another. Take the alphabet: twenty-six elements we all can recite in perfect order — A, B, C, D... But what is the seventeenth letter? Even though we know the order, we cannot tell the absolute position without counting.")* During this speech, **freely shuffle the bottom (face) half of the deck. Give the deck one perfect faro shuffle** and have spectators cut a few times. **Cut the last card of your memorized set to the face.** The thirteen memorized cards are now every fourth card from the top.

Take another sip. Hold the deck at eye level and **very quickly riffle through the cards once**. *"Done!"*

Have someone name any number from 1–52. If they name a number divisible by 4 (4, 8, 12, 16...), simply call out the card at that position (dividing the number by four gives the card's stack number). If not: **mentally count to the next higher number divisible by four, shift the appropriate number of cards from top to bottom**, and name the corresponding card. Examples: 18 → shift two cards to the bottom (20 − 18 = 2), name the fifth card of your stack (20 ÷ 4 = 5). 33 → shift three cards, name the ninth card.

Have a spectator count the cards face-up one by one (stud-style) to confirm. Retrieve the deck and, if necessary, **cut the shifted cards back to the top**, leaving the last card of your stack on the face. Memorized cards are still every fourth.

Phase III — Finale!
*("And of course, this is really neat if you actually play cards! I always have a glass of orange juice at the card table.")* **Have a spectator deal the whole deck into four face-down hands**, thirteen cards each. As he deals, explain: *"If someone remembered the order of all cards after the shuffle, he'd know every single card on his opponents' hands after the deal. He'd simply have to recall the first card of every hand and from there, every fourth card."* — you are telling the actual method, but it sounds so inconceivable there's nothing to worry about.

Have him pick up the hand he dealt to himself (the fourth hand — instruct his dealing positions: first card to his left, second opposite, third to his right, "and one hand for yourself" directly in front). **Nonchalantly push the other three hands together and to the side** — this prevents challenges to name those cards as well.

Prepare to name the cards, then suddenly hesitate. *("Oh, of course. I forgot.")* Empty the glass. Ask the spectator to look at the face card of his packet and confidently name the last (thirteenth) card of your stack. You will be wrong. Think again, weakly repeat. *("No problem — the order was reversed during the deal. All I have to do is name every fourth card — backwards!")* **In fact, simply call out your thirteen memorized cards in order.** Have the spectator place each card face-up on the table as you name it. Build a crescendo, standing up for the last card.

*("One round of orange juice!")*

Key Notes:
- The orange juice premise is deliberate: without it, spectators might believe you genuinely possess a photographic memory — impressive, but not magical. The premise ensures they know it's "impossible," which is essential for experiencing magic (like an optical illusion — you see distorted lines but know they are parallel).
- In Phase I, the card should ideally be taken from the left (arranged) half. Use a biased spread and ask a spectator on your left. If a card is taken from the right half and replaced within it, you cannot identify it — in that case, take "another sip" and have the card moved further.
- In Phase II, freely shuffling the bottom twenty-six cards (which are not in memorized order) before the faro is completely safe and adds conviction.
- The "reversed during the deal" byplay in the Finale turns a genuine miss into a strong convincer — and cleverly explains why you need to call the cards in order.
- Having the spectator take his "own" hand: by defining the dealing positions and saying "one hand for yourself," then gesturing towards that pile when saying "pick one hand," the spectator almost invariably takes the fourth pile.
